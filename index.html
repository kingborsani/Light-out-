Will the lights stay on?

Texas runs its own power grid, ERCOT. It just set an all-time demand record of 91.1 gigawatts on July 22, 2026. In 2021 it nearly collapsed in a freeze. Move the sliders to see what keeps the power on for more than 27 million Texas customers, and what leaves millions in the dark.

The decision

You are a commissioner on the Public Utility Commission of Texas, the agency that oversees ERCOT and writes the rules for weatherizing power plants. Money and political capital are limited, so you have to choose where to push next: harden power plants and the gas supply against extreme weather, add more batteries, or slow down the wave of new data-center demand. Climate change keeps raising summer peaks while you decide. This page lets you test each choice against heat waves and freezes.

What's going on, in plain language

The issue

Electricity has to be made at the same moment it's used. Texas has only small links to other grids, so when demand outruns supply, ERCOT has to cut power to whole neighborhoods on purpose (rolling outages). Otherwise the grid could collapse for weeks. Extreme heat and extreme cold are when that's most likely.

The course concept

Risk = Hazard × Exposure × Vulnerability

Hazard is the temperature. Exposure is how much demand the grid has to serve. Vulnerability is how easily the power plants fail. Batteries are an adaptation that helps with some hazards but not others. Notice that the risk multiplies: the same freeze can be a non-event or a disaster depending on the other sliders.

Why freezes are so dangerous: correlated failure

In a freeze, the cold raises demand (60% of Texas homes heat with electricity) and knocks out supply (frozen equipment, gas wells that stop flowing, iced-up wind turbines), all at the same moment. In February 2021, 52 GW of generation (48.6% of installed capacity) was forced offline at the worst point, and ERCOT cut 20 GW of load.

What to notice when you move the sliders

  1. Summer trouble comes after sunset, not at the afternoon peak. Pick the summer heat wave, turn the temperature up to ~95 °F, and drag the batteries to 0. The tightest hours land around 7–10 p.m., when solar fades but air conditioners are still running. ERCOT sees the same pattern: its riskiest hours are now 8–10 p.m. Now add batteries back and watch the evening gap close.
  2. Weatherization is the whole story in a freeze. Pick the winter freeze. At 32 °F the weatherization slider barely matters. Below about 20 °F it decides whether the lights stay on, which is hazard × vulnerability in action. In the cliff chart below, the orange curve (2021-era equipment) falls off the cliff at a much warmer temperature than the blue one.
  3. Batteries are built for short peaks, not long freezes. In summer, batteries refill every afternoon from cheap solar and cover the evening. Now set Uri-level cold and hover over the morning hours: the batteries are drained by about 8 a.m. On a freezing day there's little spare power to refill them. This model simulates only one day, starting with batteries half full, so it is generous to batteries. Uri lasted most of a week.
  4. New demand moves the cliff to milder weather. Add 20 GW of new demand with no new power plants and an ordinary record-heat day becomes rolling outages. Exposure turns a weather event that used to be normal into a disaster.
  5. Climate change pushes the summer hazard slider to the right. Each extra 1 °F on a hot day adds about 1.6 GW of demand, roughly 400,000 homes' worth. Extreme cold is getting rarer as Texas warms, but it hasn't gone away, and more homes now heat with electricity. So a freeze that does happen hits harder than it used to.

The climate connection

☀️ Hotter summers

The Texas State Climatologist expects 100-degree days to be about four times as common by 2036 as in the 1970s–80s, and finds that extreme heat has become more frequent and more severe. On this page, a record-heat day (≈91–92 °F statewide average) moves toward what a "normal hot day" looks like as the climate warms.

❄️ Fewer, but not zero, freezes

The same report finds that extreme cold has become less frequent and less severe as Texas warms, and says events like February 2021 are extremely rare. Rare doesn't mean impossible, though. Lee & Dessler (2022) showed that ERCOT's forecasts, built only on recent weather, badly underestimated the 2021 demand. They estimated it actually peaked near 82 GW.

How the model works

This is a deliberately simple, transparent model of one 24-hour day on the ERCOT grid, rebuilt from public numbers. It's an educational model, not ERCOT's. ERCOT runs thousands of weather years, unit by unit. Here, every number comes from the sources listed below or is a stated assumption.

Temperature is the population-weighted statewide daily average, the measure Lee & Dessler (2022) used to link weather to ERCOT demand. Daily averages run well below afternoon highs. On July 22, 2023, a record-demand stretch, it was 91.5 °F, against a long-term normal of 85.8 °F. Uri's coldest day averaged about 12 °F across Texas.

Peak demand (summer) = 94.5 GW + 1.6 GW/°F × (T − 92 °F) ← ≈ the Jul 22 2026 record before demand response Peak demand (winter) = 59.9 + 0.1115 × (60 − T)^1.5 GW ← ≈ 97 GW at Uri's 12 °F (ERCOT's own estimate) Hourly demand = peak × typical hourly shape + new demand Gas/coal/nuclear out (winter) = 6% + (1 − weatherization) × 27% × cold-stress(T) ← 33% at Uri with 0% weatherization Gas/coal/nuclear out (summer) = 7% + small heat penalty (≈10% on a 92 °F day) Solar, wind = installed GW × typical hourly output (wind loses up to ~half to icing in deep cold) Batteries = charge from spare power; discharge (2-hour) saved for the tightest hours Rolling outages = demand − supply − 3.3 GW flexible loads − 2.5 GW emergency tools + 1.5 GW minimum reserve

Status rules. "Lights stay on" means at least 2.5 GW to spare. "Emergency" means reserves dropped below 2.5 GW (ERCOT's EEA level 1) but emergency tools and large flexible loads covered the gap. "Rolling outages" means reserves would fall below 1.5 GW even after those tools, which is when ERCOT orders controlled outages.

Calibration checks. With 0% weatherization at 12 °F, the model knocks out 33% of gas/coal/nuclear capacity, the same share ERCOT reported for Uri. At 92 °F with no batteries it shows an evening emergency with no outages, like the September 2023 emergency. With today's settings at Uri's temperature it shows large rolling outages. ERCOT itself put the chance of controlled outages at 54% if a 2021-scale freeze happened in winter 2025–26. NERC's extreme-cold scenario showed a ~15 GW shortfall.

Limits. The model ignores transmission bottlenecks, imports over the small DC ties, multi-day storms (Uri lasted most of a week), and the uncertainty in every number. It assumes every working plant is available when needed. Treat the outputs as "about this big," not as forecasts.
